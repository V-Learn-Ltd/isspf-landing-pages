Look At The Keepers You Coach This Season.

There's a twelve-year-old who plays everything off his right foot, because nothing that's reached him has ever asked for his left.

There's a thirteen-year-old who's suddenly taller than you, still doing work built for the body he had a year ago. And there's another in the same age group who hasn't started growing yet. He's the smallest in the group, and he's begun to notice.

And there's the fifteen-year-old who's the best keeper at the club in training and someone else on match day. You've watched him make a save all week, then seen the same save go missing at the weekend. "Confidence" has never felt like enough of an answer.

You're not short of drills for any of them. You could run sessions for weeks without repeating one. They come from where drills come from now: a YouTube channel you rate, a reel someone sent you, a session-plan site, the coach on the next pitch, what worked for you when you were the one in goal.

Every one of those gave you the drill. Very few told you why that work belongs at that age and not the one before. Or what it's building towards. Or what to change when the keeper in front of you doesn't fit the plan. That part gets lost somewhere between whoever designed the drill and your pitch.

Most weeks you get by. The sessions run, the keepers work hard, and a busy goalkeeping area doesn't get questioned.

Then someone asks. A parent wants to know why his lad is doing that work at his age. The head coach wants to know why the keepers are off on their own again. You give a fair answer, and you know how far it goes. One more "why" and you're back to "it's a good drill".

And there's a cost that takes years to surface. A keeper can do the wrong work at the wrong age, season after season, and look fine the whole way through. By the time it shows, he's moved up an age group, maybe to another coach. There's no going back to find where it went wrong.

In The Goalkeeper Science Report, a sporting director presses a coach on the order of his drills. That was one session. Your version of his question runs across years: why this work, at this age, for this keeper.

Anyone can show you the drill. We make sure you can explain it.

You've Bought Coaching Courses Before.

Badges. Goalkeeping modules. Weekend courses. An online programme or two. Every one of them promised to make you a better coach, and plenty of what they taught is still in your sessions.

Look at what you came away with for your keepers, though. Technique, a framework, a handful of sessions. Much less on why a keeper of 12 needs one kind of work and a keeper of 15 needs another.

That comes down to what those courses are there to do. A goalkeeping badge sets a standard for every goalkeeper coach who takes it, at every level of the game. A general youth course has to cover every position at once. Neither has room to follow one keeper year after year: how he grows, how he handles match day, how he learns a skill. So you came back to your club with the headings. The calls under each one were left to you.

That's the part this course is built for. It follows one position, the goalkeeper, through his youth years. There are lectures on how he grows, how he copes with pressure and how he learns a skill. Every one is taught by a lecturer from academy and development football.

Now Take Those Same Keepers Through The Course.

Each lecturer here is introduced with roles he has held during his career.

The fifteen-year-old: brilliant in training, someone else in matches.

Daniel Tumelty-Bevan has been head of academy goalkeeping at Birmingham City. He teaches how goalkeeping skills are learned, through experience and perception. He also compares repeating a technique with practice that looks like the game. He makes the point with a Russian Matryoshka doll: the situation holds the keeper's position the way the outer doll holds the one inside.

Jeff Richardson has coached goalkeepers in Brentford's academy. He takes the other half: the keeper who looks sharp in training and struggles once the match starts. He covers emotion and anxiety, and the difference between a challenge state and a threat state. He also covers how perception and arousal can be changed, and how a keeper prepares mentally before a game.

Put those two lectures side by side and you know what to change in his next session. Often it's the same drill with different practice around it, practice that looks more like his Saturday. And the word you have with him before kick-off has something behind it.

The one who's shot up, and the one who hasn't started.

At Singapore's national development centre, Alex Segovia Vilchez has led the performance side. He gives you a development model for physical qualities. He shows where mechanical and functional activities fit, and how exercise progresses as a keeper moves up the age groups. When your thirteen-year-old comes back taller, you'll have an order for his physical work instead of a guess.

Jack Christopher's years in Chelsea's academy took him to head of performance support. He covers nutrition through each phase of development, and how it supports growth and maturation. When a parent asks what a growing keeper needs, you've got somewhere solid to start.

A keeper of 12 and one of 15 aren't the same athlete. Two lads in the same age group can be a long way apart in their growth, too. These two lectures show you what that means for the work you set each of them, the tall one and the small one.

The twelve-year-old and his right foot.

Ivan Kovčić worked with Dinamo Zagreb's goalkeepers and latterly headed the club's academy goalkeeping programme. He teaches individual against team development: when a keeper trains on his own, when he trains with the team, and how to spot problems through football situations. Then Dani Ortiz shows how FC Barcelona's academy builds distribution into its young keepers, starting with the foot they'd rather not use.

And the plan across every age.

Eduardo Cachulo has worked in NEOM's goalkeeping academy and spent a season as an assistant in senior goalkeeping at Real Valladolid. He covers the key stages in a young goalkeeper's development and the markers for success in the position. He also covers where the goalkeeper coach sits inside a technical department, and the step from youth football to the senior professional game.

Graeme Smith played the game professionally and has worked in coach education and goalkeeping performance at the Scottish FA. He covers the goalkeeping curriculum and the kinds of goalkeeper analysis used in developmental football.

At Kilmarnock, Fraser Stewart has been head of goalkeeping, and he has worked in coach education with the Scottish FA. He covers what it means to be a specialist goalkeeping coach, and what that coach has to weigh up on the training pitch and in the game.

Between them, those three cover the whole run, from a keeper's early stages to the step up to senior football.

Barcelona's Academy Distribution Work, Taught By Someone Who Coached Inside It.

Barcelona gets quoted in coaching all the time. Dani Ortiz has coached there.

His roles have included coaching goalkeepers in FC Barcelona's academy. He has also worked with FC Andorra's first-team goalkeepers and set pieces. In his lecture he teaches the academy's distribution work first-hand, from the young keepers he coached there.

Four things you'll watch him teach:

  • The light of the ball. Barcelona treats the ball like a torch: where it is decides how every player relates to the others. The idea comes from the club's director of methodology, and Ortiz says so.
  • The traffic light. Young keepers judge their own performance the way a traffic light works: green, amber or red. Ask a twelve-year-old how his distribution went, and he says "amber", and tells you why.
  • The weaker foot. The aim is a young keeper bold enough to treat his non-dominant foot as a real option. In the lecture, a keeper called Max carries the ball out and finds a teammate far up the pitch with his left foot.
  • Beating a high press. The training situations for it, how keeper and outfielders solve it together in game situations, and the academy's approach to distribution on match day.

Think of your twelve-year-old and his right foot. Ortiz shows the coaching behind the ball Max plays, and once you've seen it, you can decide where that work belongs with your own keepers, and say why you put it there.

How The Course Handles Age.

The lectures follow the calls that change as a keeper grows. Eduardo Cachulo covers the key stages in his development. Alex Segovia Vilchez covers how exercise progresses through the youth years. Jack Christopher covers nutrition as he grows and matures. Ivan Kovčić covers when a keeper trains on his own and when he trains with the team.

None of it comes filed under U10 or U14, and that's the useful way round. The course keeps coming back to the differences between individual keepers. That's what your thirteen-year-old who's shot up needs from you, and so does the one who hasn't started. You decide what goes at each age. The lectures give you the grounds for each decision, so the plan is your own, with something solid under every part of it.

Questions Coaches Ask Before They Enrol.

Do I need to coach at an academy?
No. It's for any coach who works with young goalkeepers, whatever the club. The lecturers draw on their academy work, and what they cover, from growth and pressure to skill learning and distribution, is part of coaching a young keeper wherever he plays.
How is it organised?
Around the calls a coach makes as a keeper moves up through the ages: development stages, growth, training design, skill learning, pressure and distribution. You decide what goes at each age for your own keepers. The lectures give you the grounds for it.
How does the course work?
You study online, when it suits you, with no deadlines and no live classes. The lectures are in English. Subtitles are there if you want them, in English, Spanish, French, Dutch, German or Italian. Each lecture has reading references either side of it and a short multiple-choice test once you've watched it. At the end there's a tutor-assessed assignment.
Is there a group or live element?
You won't find a cohort, live calls or a forum. The lectures fit around your own week.

Yes. You receive a Certificate of Achievement once your assessments reach the 75% grade.
Does the certificate count with clubs and federations?
ISSPF courses are accredited or endorsed by football federations and universities. The current list is at isspf.com/accreditations. This is a professional certificate. It sits alongside UEFA, FA and federation licences rather than replacing one, and it isn't a degree.
Isn't a lot of this free on YouTube?
Plenty of good drills are, yes. A clip shows you the session. It rarely tells you where that session sits in a young keeper's development, or why it goes there and not earlier. The nine lecturers teach that part. You'll find sessions inside the lectures, but if a bank of drills is what you're after, there's plenty of that elsewhere.
Is it mostly sports science rather than goalkeeping?
The goalkeeping lectures cover the goalkeeper coach's role, the young keeper's long-term development and his role in the game. Others cover training him alone and with the team, analysing him, and distribution at FC Barcelona's academy. Three lectures sit beside those: Jack Christopher on youth nutrition and growth, Jeff Richardson on managing pressure, and Daniel Tumelty-Bevan on skill acquisition. They're there because growth and match-day pressure both shape what you can ask of a keeper at each age.
Do I need a sports science background?
No. It's a course on coaching decisions, taught by coaches and practitioners from academy and development football.
Will it sort a keeper out quickly?
It gives you grounds for every change you make to a keeper's work. The keeper still needs time, and it won't sort him out by Saturday. No course can promise a keeper's career or a coach's next job, and this one doesn't.
