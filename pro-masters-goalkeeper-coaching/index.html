The cross your keeper trained twice this month

You ran both crossing sessions this month, and they went well.

On Monday the head coach or manager goes back over the second goal. He stops at the moment the ball is struck and asks why your keeper started from there.

You give him something about decision-making. It's true enough. He nods and moves on to the next goal, and his question stays unanswered.

That evening you open next week's plan. The crossing session stays as it is. You'd change it tomorrow if you knew what to change it to.

On Tuesday your keeper asks what you're working on. You tell him crosses.

The Monday question was about one starting position. Now ask it of every day on your plan. Why was Tuesday's session on a Tuesday? What did Friday's work have to do with the team you were about to play? After the final whistle, what did you check the plan against?

Your sessions come from YouTube breakdowns, reels other coaches send you, the coach on the next pitch, a course or two and your own years in goal. Plenty of it is good, and you've made most of it work.

Each drill reached you on its own, without the plan around it. You never saw why it sat on that day, for that keeper, against that opponent.

So your keeper gets a run of good sessions that were never built to add up to a match. And when the match shows you something, all you can reach for is the next drill.

In the Goalkeeper Science Report, the Five-Drill Test asked how many of your go-to drills you could explain. It took them one at a time, which is the right place to start. This course takes the days those drills sit in, and shows you how a goalkeeper coach plans them.

Lee Baxter's Tuesday was an away game in Angola

Lee Baxter is a UEFA A goalkeeper coach. He's worked in Turkey, Denmark and South Africa, including national team duty. In his case-study module he opens up how he builds a keeper's week. The first thing he looks at is the next opponent.

How do they score? Cutbacks? Crosses, and down which side? Is there a player who likes to come inside and finish? Each answer can become a session.

His crossing sessions have opposition players and tackle bags in the box. That's down to the league. There's a lot of body contact, and defenders don't screen the keeper as well as he'd like.

Go back to your cross. Twice this month in training. Did either session have bodies in the box, bumping and blocking the way the match did?

He asks whether his keepers are doing too much or too little, and how much work goes in when there are two games in a week. Then he lays one of those weeks out:

  • Monday: the pre-match session, with crossing, distribution, some shot-stopping and work on timing.
  • Tuesday: away in Angola.
  • The next day: off.
  • Thursday: what he calls a "technical session plus", pitched a bit above normal intensity.
  • Friday: shot-stopping on angles. Sunday's opponent likes to cut inside and finish through the inside and central channels.
  • Sunday: derby day.

After the derby, he goes back to the aims he set at the start of the week. Did they show up in the game?

Take Friday. It's on the plan because of how Sunday's opponent scores. Ask why Friday looked the way it did, and the answer is one sentence long.

His games fell on a Tuesday and a Sunday that week. Yours might be a Saturday match and a couple of evening sessions before it. His questions work for that week too. How does Saturday's opponent score? How much work goes in? Which evening does each session go on? What will you check after the match? You can ask them of any fixture list, at any club.

The heaviest work your keeper does all week

Matt Newton has worked as Lead Academy Sports Scientist at Cardiff City. In his module he looks at goalkeepers through GPS data. Keepers made more explosive efforts, more high-intensity changes of direction and more jumps in training than in games. They did cover more distance in games. Newton calls the goalkeeper the one position that does more work in training than in the match. The training work is more physically demanding too.

So the heaviest physical work your keeper does all week isn't on match day. It's in sessions you choose.

Newton also sets out why the high-intensity work comes early in the week and eases off towards the end. Easing off cuts fatigue and freshens the keeper up. If nobody decides where that work goes, it ends up wherever there's room. That might be the day before the match.

Put the two lecturers side by side. Baxter builds each day from how the next opponent scores. Newton shows where the heavy work belongs, and why it eases off before the game. Which drill you pick is one decision. The others are which day it goes on, how much of it your keeper does, and what it's there to set up. Those are the ones this course teaches.

What changes in your week

The course is built to change one thing first: the plan you write for your keeper.

  • A session falls flat on Tuesday, and you know what to change on Thursday, because you know what Tuesday was for.
  • Your keeper does in the match what you worked on in the week, and when it shows up, you can point to the session it came from.
  • Every training day has a job you could write in one line. You chose it before the session, not after.
  • A week with two games gets a plan of its own. You've watched a working goalkeeper coach decide how much goes in. Now you've got his questions to decide it yourself.
  • When you're asked why your keeper did what he did, your answer comes from the plan you wrote the Monday before.
  • When a keeper who trained well goes missing on match day, you've got more places to look than the session. There's fuel, in Dr Liam Anderson's dietary case study of an EPL goalkeeper. And there's pressure, with Jeff Richardson and Dr Tanja Simone Ecken.
